02 / THE BIG QUESTION

What does density
actually tell us?

[ CONTEXT OVER HEADLINES ]
603×

Tower Hamlets was around 603 times as densely populated as Powys in 2021.

ONS recorded 15,695 residents per km² in Tower Hamlets and just 26 per km² in Powys. But this does not mean the first authority needs 603 times as many libraries, social care staff or bus routes.

Density is useful context. Planning also needs actual population distribution, travel time, deprivation, age, housing, the existing estate and service usage. National averages cannot stand in for a local decision.

WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS
Applying authority-wide average density to a perfectly circular area assumes uniform population, which real places do not have.

Model: people = Census density × π × radius². Participants = people × user-selected participation rate. Hubs = ceiling(participants ÷ user-selected hub capacity). No actual service demand, budgets, accessibility measures, demand forecasts, travel networks or ward population grids are included. Treat the output as a lesson in assumptions, not a service recommendation.

04 / THE ANALYTICAL METHOD

Make the logic
visible.

Each stage is reproducible, from the published Census observation to the user-controlled experiment.

[ METHOD / LIMITATIONS ]
01 / DEFINE

Choose a consistent unit.

Use persons per km², with the same reference year of Census 2021. These are selected authorities, not a national ranking or all-current council boundaries.

02 / VISUALISE

Connect data to geography.

Use a simplified UK silhouette and approximate authority-centre markers. The visual bars encode log-transformed density, not map height or real buildings.

03 / EXPERIMENT

Separate evidence from assumptions.

Observed population density is the only real-world numerical input in the planner. Radius, participation, capacity and resulting requirements are hypothetical.
